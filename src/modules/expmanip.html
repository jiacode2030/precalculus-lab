Exponent rules & rewriting

The same exponential can be written many ways. Exponent rules show that shifting is stretching, and that changing the step length changes the base.

The idea

Exponent rules: bᵐ·bⁿ = bᵐ⁺ⁿ, bᵐ/bⁿ = bᵐ⁻ⁿ, (bᵐ)ⁿ = bᵐⁿ, b⁻ⁿ = 1/bⁿ, b⁰ = 1 and b^(1/n) = ⁿ√b. Every rule just counts how many copies of b are being multiplied.

A horizontal shift of an exponential is a vertical stretch: a·b^(x+k) = (a·bᵏ)·bˣ. For example 3·2^(x+2) = 3·4·2ˣ = 12·2ˣ. So the graph of every exponential a·b^(x−h) can be written with no shift, just a different initial value. This is special to exponentials: a shifted parabola cannot be rewritten as a stretched one.

Changing the length of a step changes the base: b^(x/k) = (b^(1/k))ˣ. A quantity that doubles every 3 years is 2^(x/3) = (2^(1/3))ˣ ≈ (1.26)ˣ, about 26% growth per year. Half-life problems use base ½ with the half-life as the step: A(t) = A₀(½)^(t/T).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Set a = 1.5, b = 2 and h = −2. Which unshifted form is the same graph?

    What you should see

    a·b^(−h) = 1.5·4 = 6, so 6·2ˣ. The two graphs overlap completely.

  2. Change h to 2. Does the stretch factor grow or shrink?

    What you should see

    It shrinks to 1.5/4 = 0.375: shifting right by 2 is the same as scaling down by 2² = 4.

  3. In the second lab choose doubles with T = 3. What is the yearly factor?

    What you should see

    2^(1/3) ≈ 1.26: about 26% growth per unit. After 3 units the factor is 1.26³ = 2, as it should be.

  4. Choose halves with T = 5. What fraction is lost each unit?

    What you should see

    The per-unit factor is (½)^(1/5) ≈ 0.871, so about 12.9% is lost each unit.

Worked example: shift or stretch?

A model for monthly sales (in thousands of items) is S(t) = 3·2^(t + 2), where t is the number of months after the product launch. Rewrite it in the form a·2ᵗ and say what S(0) means.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Use the product rule. 2^(t + 2) = 2ᵗ·2² = 4·2ᵗ.
  2. Multiply by the 3. S(t) = 3·4·2ᵗ = 12·2ᵗ.
  3. Check. S(1) = 3·2³ = 24 and 12·2¹ = 24 ✓.
  4. Meaning. S(0) = 12 thousand items in the launch month, and sales double every month. The shift of 2 months was hiding the initial value.

Worked example 2: half-life

A sample of 80 grams of a radioactive isotope has a half-life of 5 days. Write A(t) for the grams left after t days, find the percent lost per day, and find the amount after 12 days.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Model. Half the amount remains after each 5 days, so A(t) = 80(½)^(t/5).
  2. Daily factor. (½)^(t/5) = ((½)^(1/5))ᵗ = (0.8706…)ᵗ. The factor per day is about 0.8706, so about 12.9% is lost each day.
  3. After 12 days. A(12) = 80(½)^(12/5) = 80(½)^2.4 = 80(0.1895…) ≈ 15.16 grams.
  4. Check. 12 days is between 10 days (20 g) and 15 days (10 g), and 15.16 is between 10 and 20 ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Writing 2^(x+2) = 2ˣ + 4. The exponents add when you multiply: 2^(x+2) = 2ˣ·2².
  • Using (bᵐ)ⁿ = bᵐ⁺ⁿ. It is bᵐⁿ.
  • Saying a doubling time of 3 years means 2/3 = 67% growth per year. It means 2^(1/3) ≈ 1.26.
  • Forgetting that b⁻ⁿ is a small positive number, not a negative number.

Why shifts are stretches only for exponentials

f(x) = bˣ turns addition in the input into multiplication in the output: f(x + k) = f(x)·f(k). That is why a shift of the graph left by k just multiplies every height by bᵏ. No other family of functions does this, which is the algebraic reason exponentials are the model for constant percent change.
