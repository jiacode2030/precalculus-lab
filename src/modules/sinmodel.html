Sinusoidal models

Real data that rises and falls on a schedule can be modeled with a sinusoid. Read four numbers from the data and the model writes itself.

The idea

To model periodic data with f(t) = a cos(b(t − h)) + d, read the features straight from the data: the midline d = (max + min)/2, the amplitude |a| = (max − min)/2, the period P (so b = 2π/P), and the time of a maximum h (cosine starts at its maximum, so it is the natural choice). If it is easier to start at a minimum, use a = −|a|; if the data starts on the midline going up, use sine.

A model is a simplification. Check it against the data: the vertical gaps between the data and the model (the residuals) should be small and show no pattern. A model is most trustworthy between the data points (interpolation) and over a few cycles; far outside the data the real situation can change.

In context, every parameter has units and a meaning: d is the average level, |a| how far it swings, P how long one cycle takes, h when the peak happens. AP questions often ask you to interpret one of them or the value of the model at a time.

Worked example: hours of daylight

The table gives the hours of daylight at 40° N latitude on the 21st of each month (day 21, 52, 80, …, 355). The longest is 14.84 h on day 172 (June 21) and the shortest is 9.16 h on day 355 (December 21). Build a sinusoidal model, then use it to estimate how many days per year have more than 14 hours of daylight.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Max and min. 14.84 h at day 172 and 9.16 h at day 355.
  2. Midline and amplitude. d = (14.84 + 9.16)/2 = 12 and |a| = (14.84 − 9.16)/2 = 2.84.
  3. Period. The cycle is one year: P = 365 days, so b = 2π/365. (Check: max to min is 183 days, about half a year.)
  4. Model. Start at the maximum with cosine: D(t) = 12 + 2.84 cos(2π(t − 172)/365).
  5. More than 14 h. 2.84 cos(…) > 2 gives cos(2π(t − 172)/365) > 0.704, so |t − 172| < (365/2π)·arccos(0.704) ≈ 45.9. That is day 126 to day 218 (about May 6 to August 6): about 92 days. The sun's actual position gives 87 days: close, but a reminder that the model is an approximation.

Worked example 2: a wind turbine blade

A wind turbine's hub is 80 m above the ground and each blade is 40 m long. The rotor turns once every 4 seconds. At t = 0 a blade tip is level with the hub and rising. (a) Write h(t), the tip's height. (b) Find h(1.5). (c) For how long during each turn is the tip above 100 m?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Features. Midline = hub height 80 m; amplitude = blade length 40 m; period 4 s, so b = 2π/4 = π/2.
  2. (a) Which function? It starts on the midline going up, so use sine: h(t) = 80 + 40 sin(πt/2).
  3. Check. At t = 1 (a quarter turn) h = 80 + 40 = 120 m, the top. ✓
  4. (b) h(1.5) = 80 + 40 sin(3π/4) = 80 + 20√2 ≈ 108.3 m.
  5. (c) 80 + 40 sin(πt/2) > 100 gives sin(πt/2) > 1/2, so π/6 < πt/2 < 5π/6 and 1/3 < t < 5/3. That is 4/3 s of every 4-second turn: one third of the time.

Common mistakes

  • Using the period itself as b. b = 2π/P.
  • Reading the midline as the starting value. It is (max + min)/2.
  • Using sine when the data starts at a maximum, then fighting with a phase shift. Pick the function whose starting point matches.
  • Judging a fit by one point. Look at all the residuals: small and patternless is the goal.
  • Trusting the model far beyond the data (decades of daylight is fine; decades of stock prices is not).

Why cosine is usually easier for data

Data tables almost always show the maximum and the minimum clearly; the midline crossing is between data points and harder to pin down. Cosine with a = +|a| starts at a maximum, so the phase h is just "when is it biggest". If the minimum is clearer, use −cos and the time of the minimum.
