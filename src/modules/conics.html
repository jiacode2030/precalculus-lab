Conic sections

Slice a cone and you get four curves. Their equations differ in just the squared terms: one square gives a parabola, two squares added give a circle or an ellipse, two squares subtracted give a hyperbola.

The idea

The four conic sections have standard forms. Circle: (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r². Ellipse: (x − h)²/a² + (y − k)²/b² = 1, centered (h, k) with semi-axes a (horizontal) and b (vertical). Hyperbola: (x − h)²/a² − (y − k)²/b² = 1 opens left and right (swap the terms to open up and down). Parabola: (x − h)² = 4p(y − k), vertex (h, k), opening up (p > 0) or down.

Each has a distance rule built on special points. An ellipse is all points whose distances to two foci add to a constant (2a). A hyperbola is all points whose distances to the foci differ by a constant (2a). A parabola is all points equally far from the focus and a line, the directrix. Where the foci are: ellipse c² = |a² − b²| along the longer axis; hyperbola c² = a² + b²; parabola the focus is p from the vertex.

To read a conic from a general equation, complete the square in x and in y to reach a standard form. The hyperbola's branches approach the asymptotes y − k = ±(b/a)(x − h) (for the left-right form), which are the diagonals of the box a by b around the center.

Name the conic

Look at the squared terms: how many, which signs, which coefficients?

1. The equation is x² + y² = 16. Which conic is it?

Both squared, same sign, same coefficient: a circle of radius 4.

2. The equation is x²/25 + y²/9 = 1. Which conic is it?

Both squared, same sign, different denominators: an ellipse with semi-axes 5 and 3.

3. The equation is x² − y²/4 = 1. Which conic is it?

The squared terms have opposite signs: a hyperbola, opening left and right (the x² term is positive).

4. The equation is x² = 6y. Which conic is it?

Only one variable is squared: a parabola with 4p = 6, so p = 1.5. It opens upward.

5. The equation is 4x² + y² = 36. Which conic is it?

Both squared and added: an ellipse. Dividing by 36 gives x²/9 + y²/36 = 1: semi-axes 3 and 6.

6. The equation is y²/9 − x²/4 = 1. Which conic is it?

Opposite signs: a hyperbola. The y² term is positive, so it opens up and down.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose Ellipse, Horizontal, a = 3 and b = 2. Where are the foci, and why are they on the x-axis?

    What you should see

    c² = a² − b² = 9 − 4 = 5, so the foci are (±√5, 0) ≈ (±2.24, 0): on the longer axis, which is horizontal because a is larger.

  2. Make a = b on the ellipse. What happens to the foci?

    What you should see

    c = 0: the two foci merge at the center and the ellipse is a circle.

  3. Choose Hyperbola. Where are the asymptotes, and what do they do to the shape of the branches?

    What you should see

    They are the lines y = ±(2/3)x through the center, the diagonals of the 3-by-2 box. The branches bend toward them and never touch them.

  4. In the second lab choose the ellipse and drag the point all the way around. What stays the same?

    What you should see

    The sum of the two distances to the foci is always 10 = 2a: it is the ellipse's defining rule.

Worked example: an ellipse from a general equation

Identify the conic 9x² + 4y² − 36x + 24y + 36 = 0 and find its center, vertices and foci.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Group and complete the square. 9(x² − 4x) + 4(y² + 6y) = −36 → 9(x − 2)² − 36 + 4(y + 3)² − 36 = −36, so 9(x − 2)² + 4(y + 3)² = 36. Divide by 36: (x − 2)²/4 + (y + 3)²/9 = 1, an ellipse.
  2. Center and vertices. Center (2, −3). The semi-axes are a = 2 (horizontal) and b = 3 (vertical). Vertices: (2 ± 0, −3 ± 3) = (2, 0), (2, −6) and (2 ± 2, −3) = (0, −3), (4, −3).
  3. Foci. The longer axis is vertical (3 > 2), so c² = 9 − 4 = 5 and c = √5 ≈ 2.236. Foci: (2, −3 ± √5) ≈ (2, −0.76) and (2, −5.24).
  4. Check. The point (2, 0): 9(0)² + 4(3)² = 36 ✓, on the curve. The distances from (2, 0) to the two foci are 0.76 and 5.24, which add to 6 = 2b ✓.

Worked example 2: a hyperbola from its vertices and foci

A hyperbola centered at the origin has vertices (±3, 0) and foci (±5, 0). Find its equation and its asymptotes.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Read a and c. The vertices are at distance a = 3 and the foci at distance c = 5. The foci are on the x-axis, so the hyperbola opens left and right.
  2. Find b. For a hyperbola c² = a² + b², so b² = 25 − 9 = 16 and b = 4.
  3. Equation. x²/9 − y²/16 = 1.
  4. Asymptotes. y = ±(b/a)x = ±(4/3)x, the diagonals of the 3-by-4 box. Check: at x = 5, y² = 16(25/9 − 1) = 256/9, so y = ±16/3 ≈ ±5.33, close to the asymptote value (4/3)(5) = 6.67 and under it ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up ellipse and hyperbola foci: ellipse c² = a² − b² (subtract), hyperbola c² = a² + b² (add).
  • Reading a and b backwards: a goes with the x² term only for the horizontal form, and for an ellipse the larger denominator is the longer axis.
  • Forgetting to divide by the constant to get 1 on the right before reading a² and b².
  • Writing the asymptote slope as a/b: for the left-right form it is b/a.
  • Thinking a parabola's two arms are asymptotic: they are not, they just spread apart.

Orbits, mirrors and radar dishes

A planet moves on an ellipse with the sun at one focus. A satellite dish is a parabola: signals parallel to its axis bounce to the focus. A hyperbola models a sonic boom's shape and the paths of some comets that pass the sun once. The foci and the distance rules are what make these curves useful.
