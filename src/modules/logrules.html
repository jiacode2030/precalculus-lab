Log rules & equivalent forms

Logarithms turn products into sums, quotients into differences and powers into multiples. Those rules are how you rewrite, condense and compare log expressions.

The idea

Because logarithms are exponents, they inherit the rules of exponents: product log_b(mn) = log_b m + log_b n (exponents add when you multiply), quotient log_b(m/n) = log_b m − log_b n, and power log_b(mᵖ) = p·log_b m. Special values: log_b 1 = 0 and log_b b = 1.

Different bases are related by change of base: log_b x = ln x / ln b = log x / log b. So the graphs of log₂ x, log₁₀ x and ln x are vertical stretches of one another. Also log_b(cx) = log_b c + log_b x: stretching a log graph horizontally is the same as shifting it vertically.

To expand an expression like log₂(8x³), use the rules: log₂ 8 + 3 log₂ x = 3 + 3 log₂ x. To condense, run them backward: 2 log₃ x + log₃ 5 − log₃ 10 = log₃(x²/2). The rules apply to products, quotients and powers: there is no rule for log_b(m + n).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. With the product rule, m = 6 and n = 4: compare the two sides.

    What you should see

    log₂ 24 and log₂ 6 + log₂ 4 are both about 4.585: the lengths of the two blue-green bars match the single orange bar.

  2. Switch to the quotient rule with m = 20, n = 5.

    What you should see

    log₂(20/5) = log₂ 4 = 2, and log₂ 20 − log₂ 5 = 4.32 − 2.32 = 2. Subtracting logs divides the numbers.

  3. Use the power rule with m = 5 and p = 3.

    What you should see

    log₂(5³) = log₂ 125 ≈ 6.97, and 3·log₂ 5 ≈ 6.97. An exponent on the inside comes out as a multiplier.

  4. In the second lab move x. Does the ratio log₂ x ÷ log₁₀ x ever change?

    What you should see

    No: it is always about 3.32 (= 1/log₁₀ 2), because log₂ x = log₁₀ x / log₁₀ 2. The graphs are vertical stretches of one another.

Worked example: condense an expression

Write 2 log₃ x + log₃ 5 − log₃ 10 as a single logarithm, and check it at x = 3.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Power rule. 2 log₃ x = log₃(x²).
  2. Product and quotient. log₃(x²) + log₃ 5 − log₃ 10 = log₃(x² · 5 / 10).
  3. Simplify. The single logarithm is log₃(x²/2).
  4. Check at x = 3. 2 log₃ 3 + log₃ 5 − log₃ 10 = 2 + (1.465 − 2.096) = 1.369, and log₃(9/2) = log₃ 4.5 = 1.369 ✓.

Worked example 2: decibels

Sound level is L = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) decibels, where I is the intensity and I₀ the quietest sound heard. By how many decibels does L change when the intensity doubles? When it is multiplied by 10?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Doubling. The new level is 10 log₁₀(2I/I₀) = 10[log₁₀ 2 + log₁₀(I/I₀)] = 10 log₁₀ 2 + L.
  2. Evaluate. 10 log₁₀ 2 = 10(0.30103) ≈ 3.01 dB: every doubling adds about 3 dB.
  3. Times 10. 10 log₁₀ 10 = 10(1) = 10 dB.
  4. Meaning. The product rule converts a multiplication of intensity into an addition of decibels, which is why decibels use logs.

Common mistakes

  • Writing log(m + n) = log m + log n. There is no sum rule; only products become sums.
  • Dropping the parentheses: log₂ x² means log₂(x²) = 2 log₂ x, not (log₂ x)².
  • Using the power rule on a product inside: log(3x)² is 2 log(3x), not 2 log 3x.
  • Changing base the wrong way: log_b x = ln x / ln b (the base goes underneath).

Why the log rules are the exponent rules in disguise

Let m = bᵃ and n = bᶜ, so a = log_b m and c = log_b n. Then mn = bᵃ·bᶜ = b^(a + c), which says log_b(mn) = a + c. The quotient rule comes from bᵃ/bᶜ = b^(a − c) and the power rule from (bᵃ)ᵖ = b^(ap).
