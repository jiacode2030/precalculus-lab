Sine & cosine graphs

Unroll the unit circle: the height of the moving point is the sine wave, its width is the cosine wave.

The idea

Run a point around the unit circle and record its height at every angle: plotted against θ, that is y = sin θ. Record its width instead and you get y = cos θ. Both repeat every 2π, both stay between −1 and 1, and both have midline y = 0.

Read the behavior straight off the circle. Going up the right side of the circle (θ from −π/2 to π/2) the height grows, so sin is increasing there; going down the left side it is decreasing. The width shrinks over the top half (0 to π), so cos is decreasing on (0, π) and increasing on (π, 2π).

Near the top and bottom of the circle the point moves sideways, so the height barely changes: the sine wave flattens at its maximum and minimum. Near the sides it moves straight up or down: the sine wave is steepest where it crosses its midline. That is why sin is concave down above the midline and concave up below it.

Symmetry: the angle −θ is the mirror image of θ across the x-axis. Same width, opposite height, so cos(−θ) = cos θ (cosine is even) and sin(−θ) = −sin θ (sine is odd).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Press Play and watch the blue line carry the height across. At which angles is the height 0? At which is it biggest?

    What you should see

    Height 0 at θ = 0, π and 2π (the point is on the x-axis). The biggest height, 1, is at θ = π/2 (top of the circle); the smallest, −1, at 3π/2.

  2. Set θ = π/2 (1.571) and nudge it a little either way. Why does the sine graph look flat there?

    What you should see

    At the top of the circle the point is moving sideways, so its height hardly changes: sin θ stays near 1. The sine graph has a turning point there and is concave down.

  3. Trace cos only and drag θ from 0 to π. What happens to the width, and to the graph?

    What you should see

    The width falls from 1 to −1 as the point goes over the top of the circle, so cos is decreasing on (0, π). It falls fastest at θ = π/2, where the point moves straight left.

  4. In the second lab, set θ = 1 with sin, then switch to cos. Compare f(θ) and f(−θ) each time.

    What you should see

    sin(1) ≈ 0.841 and sin(−1) ≈ −0.841: opposite, so sine is odd (symmetric about the origin). cos(1) ≈ 0.540 and cos(−1) ≈ 0.540: equal, so cosine is even (symmetric about the y-axis).

  5. With sin, slide θ from 0 to 1.3 and watch the secant slope on [θ, θ + π/6]. What happens to the average rate?

    What you should see

    It shrinks from about 0.955 toward 0 and then turns negative: sine is increasing but more and more slowly, the signature of concave down. On [0, π/6] it is (1/2 − 0)/(π/6) = 3/π ≈ 0.955.

Worked example

On [0, 2π], find the interval where both sin θ and cos θ are decreasing. On that interval, describe the concavity of each graph.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Sine. The height falls as the point goes down the left side of the circle: sin is decreasing on (π/2, 3π/2).
  2. Cosine. The width falls as the point goes over the top: cos is decreasing on (0, π).
  3. Both. The overlap of (π/2, 3π/2) and (0, π) is (π/2, π): quadrant II.
  4. Concavity. On (π/2, π) sine is above its midline, so it is concave down: decreasing faster and faster. Cosine is below its midline there, so it is concave up: still decreasing, but more and more slowly.

Worked example 2: a bicycle pedal

A bicycle crank is 17 cm long and the pedal axle is 28 cm above the ground. With the crank horizontal and pointing forward at θ = 0, and θ measured counterclockwise as seen from the right side, the pedal's height is h(θ) = 28 + 17 sin θ cm. (a) Find the highest and lowest pedal heights. (b) Find h(7π/6). (c) On which interval of one turn is the pedal going down? (d) Compare the average rate of change of h on [0, π/6] and on [π/3, π/2].

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Model. The pedal is a point on a circle of radius 17 around the axle, so its height is the axle height plus 17 times the sine: h(θ) = 28 + 17 sin θ, midline 28.
  2. (a) sin θ is between −1 and 1, so h is between 28 − 17 = 11 cm (at θ = 3π/2) and 28 + 17 = 45 cm (at θ = π/2).
  3. (b) sin(7π/6) = −1/2, so h = 28 − 8.5 = 19.5 cm.
  4. (c) h decreases exactly where sin θ does: π/2 < θ < 3π/2, the half turn from the top to the bottom.
  5. (d) On [0, π/6]: (36.5 − 28)/(π/6) = 51/π ≈ 16.2 cm per radian. On [π/3, π/2]: 17(1 − √3/2)/(π/6) ≈ 4.35 cm per radian. Same angle turned, much less rise near the top: the graph is concave down there.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the two: sine is the height (y), cosine is the width (x).
  • Saying sin is increasing on (0, π). It increases only to π/2, then decreases.
  • Thinking the steepest part of a sine wave is at the max. The max is flat; the steepest part is at the midline.
  • Mixing up odd and even: cosine is even (the graph is symmetric about the y-axis), sine is odd.
  • Graphing with θ in degrees but labeling the axis in radians (or the other way around).

Concavity without calculus

Compare average rates of change over equal steps. For sin on [0, π/6], [π/6, π/3], [π/3, π/2] the rises are 0.5, 0.366 and 0.134: equal steps in θ, smaller and smaller rises. Decreasing rates of change mean concave down. Below the midline the rates increase, so the graph is concave up. The points of inflection are where the wave crosses its midline.
