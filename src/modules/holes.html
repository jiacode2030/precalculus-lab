Holes

When a factor appears in both the numerator and the denominator, the graph has a single missing point instead of an asymptote.

The idea

Factor the top and the bottom of a rational function. A factor (x − c) in both means f is undefined at x = c, but it cancels everywhere else. After cancelling, the graph looks exactly like the simpler function, except for one missing point: a hole at x = c, drawn as an open circle.

To find the hole, cancel the common factor, then plug x = c into what is left. That output is the height of the open circle. The original function is undefined there, but the outputs get closer and closer to this number from both sides: limx→c f(x) exists and equals it.

Careful with multiplicity: if (x − c) appears more times in the denominator than in the numerator, some of it is left in the denominator after cancelling and x = c is still a vertical asymptote. A hole happens only when the factor is cancelled completely from the bottom.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose top 1, bottom 1. Where is the open circle, and is there an asymptote at x = h?

    What you should see

    The open circle sits at x = h with the height of the simplified function. There is no asymptote at h: the factor cancelled completely.

  2. Choose top 1, bottom 2. What happens now at x = h?

    What you should see

    Cancelling one factor leaves (x − h) in the denominator, so there is a vertical asymptote at h. No hole.

  3. Choose top 2, bottom 1. Why is there a hole and also a zero at h?

    What you should see

    One factor cancels (hole at x = h) and one is left in the numerator, so the simplified function has a zero there: the hole sits on the x-axis.

  4. In the second lab, choose (x² − 4)/(x − 2) and slide the zoom down to 0.02. What do you see?

    What you should see

    The graph is a straight line with a tiny gap at x = 2 that never closes, however far you zoom. The outputs near 2 are all close to 4.

  5. Choose 1/(x − 2) and zoom in the same way. What is different?

    What you should see

    The outputs get larger and larger: no number to settle on. That is an asymptote, not a hole.

Worked example: finding a hole

Find the hole and any vertical asymptote of f(x) = (x² − x − 6)/(x − 3). Sketch the graph.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Factor. x² − x − 6 = (x − 3)(x + 2), so f(x) = (x − 3)(x + 2)/(x − 3).
  2. Cancel. For x ≠ 3, f(x) = x + 2: a line. The factor (x − 3) is gone from the denominator, so no vertical asymptote.
  3. The hole. f(3) is undefined, but the line has height 3 + 2 = 5 there: hole at (3, 5).
  4. Sketch. Draw y = x + 2 and put an open circle at (3, 5). The outputs get closer and closer to 5 on both sides: lim f(x) = 5 as x → 3.

Worked example 2: a spreadsheet error

A shop's margin per unit at price x dollars is M(x) = (x² − 100)/(x − 10). The spreadsheet shows #DIV/0! at x = 10. What should the margin be there?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Factor. x² − 100 = (x − 10)(x + 10), so M(x) = (x − 10)(x + 10)/(x − 10).
  2. Cancel. For x ≠ 10, M(x) = x + 10.
  3. At x = 10. The graph is a line with a hole at (10, 20). The spreadsheet cannot divide by 0, but the values approach $20.
  4. Check. M(9.9) = 19.9, M(10.1) = 20.1: the margin passes smoothly through 20 on both sides.

Common mistakes

  • Cancelling terms instead of factors.
  • Saying a hole is an asymptote, or the other way around: look at what is left in the denominator.
  • Finding the hole height from the original formula (it is undefined there). Use the simplified one.
  • Forgetting to factor completely before deciding.

A hole is a removable discontinuity

If you simply define f(c) to be the missing height, the gap closes and the graph becomes one unbroken line. That is why holes are called removable. A vertical asymptote cannot be repaired by choosing one number.
