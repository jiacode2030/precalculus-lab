Logarithms & log functions

A logarithm is an exponent. The log graph is the exponential graph turned on its side, with a vertical asymptote instead of a horizontal one.

The idea

The logarithm log_b(x) = y means bʸ = x: it is the exponent you put on b to get x. So log₂ 8 = 3 because 2³ = 8, log₁₀ 0.001 = −3, and log₄ 8 = 3/2 because 4^(3/2) = 8. Because the logarithm undoes the exponential, log_b(bˣ) = x and b^(log_b x) = x. The base 10 logarithm is written log, and the base e ≈ 2.718 logarithm is the natural logarithm ln.

The function f(x) = log_b(x) is the inverse of bˣ, so its graph is the reflection of y = bˣ over y = x. It has domain x > 0 (you cannot raise a positive base to any power to get 0 or a negative number), range all real numbers, a vertical asymptote x = 0, and passes through (1, 0) and (b, 1). For b > 1 it is increasing and concave down; for 0 < b < 1 it is decreasing.

Transformations work as before: g(x) = log_b(x − h) + k has vertical asymptote x = h, domain x > h, and its x-intercept moves to x = h + b^(−k). Logarithms also compress huge ranges into small numbers, which is why pH, earthquake magnitude and decibels use them.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose base 2 and set x = 8. What is log₂ 8, and what is the reading on the t-axis?

    What you should see

    3, because 2³ = 8. The orange point sits at t = 3 on the t-axis.

  2. Slide x to 1. What is the logarithm of 1 for any base?

    What you should see

    0: b⁰ = 1 in every base, so the log graph always passes through (1, 0).

  3. Slide x below 1, to 0.25. What happens to the logarithm?

    What you should see

    It becomes negative: log₂ 0.25 = −2 since 2⁻² = 0.25. Numbers between 0 and 1 have negative logs.

  4. In the second lab set h = 2 and k = 1. Where is the asymptote and the x-intercept?

    What you should see

    The asymptote is x = 2 and the domain is x > 2. The intercept solves log₂(x − 2) = −1: x = 2 + 2⁻¹ = 2.5.

  5. Choose base 1/2. Is the graph increasing or decreasing?

    What you should see

    Decreasing: for bases between 0 and 1, the exponential decays, so its inverse falls.

Worked example: evaluate by asking "what exponent?"

Evaluate log₂ 32, log₂ (1/4) and log₄ 8 without a calculator.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. log₂ 32. 2⁵ = 32, so log₂ 32 = 5.
  2. log₂ (1/4). 2⁻² = 1/4, so log₂ (1/4) = −2. Numbers between 0 and 1 have negative logs.
  3. log₄ 8. Write both as powers of 2: 4 = 2² and 8 = 2³, so 4^(3/2) = 2³ = 8 and log₄ 8 = 3/2.
  4. Check on the graph. The point (32, 5) lies on y = log₂ x, and so do (1/4, −2) and (2, 1).

Worked example 2: the pH scale

The pH of a solution is pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the hydrogen-ion concentration in mol/L. Find the pH when [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵, and say how much more concentrated [H⁺] is at pH 4 than at pH 5.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Substitute. pH = −log₁₀(3.2 × 10⁻⁵).
  2. Estimate. 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ is between 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁴, so log₁₀ of it is between −5 and −4, and the pH is between 4 and 5.
  3. Evaluate. log₁₀(3.2 × 10⁻⁵) = log₁₀ 3.2 − 5 ≈ 0.505 − 5 = −4.495, so pH ≈ 4.49.
  4. Compare. pH 4 means [H⁺] = 10⁻⁴ and pH 5 means 10⁻⁵: pH 4 is 10 times more concentrated. Each pH step is a factor of 10.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking log(a + b) = log a + log b. Logs turn products into sums, not sums into sums.
  • Trying to take the log of 0 or a negative number: the domain is x > 0.
  • Writing log_b x = bˣ. The log is the exponent y in bʸ = x.
  • Confusing log₁₀ with ln: log is base 10, ln is base e.

Why logarithms compress scales

Each time the input is multiplied by b, the logarithm adds 1. So numbers from 1 to a million (10⁶) have base 10 logs between 0 and 6. That is why a pH scale from 0 to 14 can describe concentrations that differ by a factor of 10¹⁴, and why a log axis shows both tiny and huge values on one page.
