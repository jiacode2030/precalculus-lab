Implicit functions

Some curves are given by an equation in x and y rather than as y = f(x). Substitute to test points, solve for y when you can, and watch for two y-values at one x.

The idea

An implicitly defined curve is the set of points (x, y) that satisfy an equation such as x² + y² = 25 or xy = 4. A point is on the curve exactly when substituting its coordinates makes the equation true: (3, 4) works for the circle because 3² + 4² = 25, but (4, 4) does not because 32 ≠ 25.

Such a curve is often not a function of x: a vertical line can hit it more than once. For the circle, x = 3 gives y² = 16, so y = 4 or y = −4. Solving for y gives y = ±√(25 − x²), two functions: the upper half and the lower half. Each half passes the vertical line test. When there is no solution (for example x = 6 on this circle), the vertical line misses the curve and x is outside its domain.

To find where two curves meet, substitute one equation into the other and solve, then use the original equations to find the y-values (and check them in both). A curve can also be described by a parametrization: the circle x² + y² = 25 is x = 5 cos t, y = 5 sin t, which handles both y-values at once and gives the curve a direction.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose x² + y² = 25 and sweep the line to x = 3. How many points, and what are they?

    What you should see

    Two: (3, 4) and (3, −4). The vertical line meets the circle twice, so the circle is not the graph of a function of x.

  2. Move the line to x = 5, then x = 6 on the circle. What happens?

    What you should see

    At x = 5 the line just touches at (5, 0) (one point, y = 0). At x = 6 it misses the circle: x is outside the domain of the curve.

  3. Choose xy = 4 and sweep through x = 0. What is special there?

    What you should see

    No point at all: xy = 0 never equals 4. The curve has two branches that never reach the y-axis (the y-axis is a vertical asymptote). Every other vertical line meets it exactly once, so xy = 4 is the function y = 4/x.

  4. In the second lab choose the circle and press Try a point a few times, then nudge the point off the curve. What do the two sides do?

    What you should see

    On the curve the two sides are equal (25 = 25). Moving the point inside makes the left side smaller than 25; moving it outside makes it larger.

Worked example: two points at one x

For the curve x² + y² = 25: (a) find every point with x = 3; (b) is the curve a function of x? Write y in terms of x; (c) is (4, 3) on the curve? Is (4, 4)?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. (a) Substitute x = 3: 9 + y² = 25, so y² = 16 and y = 4 or −4. The points are (3, 4) and (3, −4).
  2. (b) One x with two y-values means it is not a function of x. Solving for y: y² = 25 − x², so y = ±√(25 − x²) with −5 ≤ x ≤ 5. The + sign is the upper half and the − sign the lower half; each half is a function.
  3. (c) (4, 3): 4² + 3² = 16 + 9 = 25 ✓, on the curve. (4, 4): 16 + 16 = 32 ≠ 25, not on it (32 > 25, so it is outside the circle).
  4. Domain. Because 25 − x² ≥ 0 is needed, x must be between −5 and 5. At x = ±5 the two halves meet at (±5, 0).

Worked example 2: where a line meets the circle

Find the points where the circle x² + y² = 25 meets the line y = x + 1.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Substitute. Replace y by x + 1: x² + (x + 1)² = 25, which is 2x² + 2x + 1 = 25.
  2. Solve. 2x² + 2x − 24 = 0, divide by 2: x² + x − 12 = 0, so (x + 4)(x − 3) = 0 and x = 3 or x = −4.
  3. Find y from the line: x = 3 gives y = 4; x = −4 gives y = −3. The points are (3, 4) and (−4, −3).
  4. Check in the circle. 3² + 4² = 25 ✓ and (−4)² + (−3)² = 16 + 9 = 25 ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Writing y = √(25 − x²) and calling that the whole circle: it is only the upper half.
  • Forgetting the ± when you take a square root to solve for y.
  • Finding x in an intersection but not matching it with the right y: use the line to find y, not the circle (which gives two values).
  • Testing a point in only one side of the equation without simplifying both.

Level curves and the idea of a curve you cannot solve for y

Some curves, like x³ + y³ = 6xy, cannot be solved neatly for y at all, yet they are perfectly good curves: a point is on one exactly when its coordinates satisfy the equation. In calculus you will find slopes of such curves without solving for y, by differentiating both sides (implicit differentiation).
