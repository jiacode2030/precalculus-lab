Polynomial functions

A polynomial can turn around only a limited number of times. Where it turns, and where its bending switches, tells you the shape.

The idea

A polynomial of degree n is f(x) = aₙxⁿ + … + a₁x + a₀ with aₙ ≠ 0. Its graph is smooth and unbroken. Where f switches from increasing to decreasing it has a relative (local) maximum; where it switches from decreasing to increasing, a relative minimum. Together these are the relative extrema, and a polynomial of degree n has at most n − 1 of them.

On an interval, the greatest output is the absolute (global) maximum and the least is the absolute minimum. Look at the relative extrema inside the interval and at the two endpoints: the largest and smallest of those values win. An endpoint can beat every turning point.

A point of inflection is where the graph switches concavity: where the rate of change switches from increasing to decreasing, or the other way. Between two real zeros of a polynomial there is always at least one relative extremum.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose Cubic. Drag the points until the graph has no turning points at all. Can you make three?

    What you should see

    No turning points is easy (put the points in rising order along a gentle curve). Three is impossible: a cubic has at most 3 − 1 = 2.

  2. With the cubic, where is the gold inflection point compared with the two orange turning points?

    What you should see

    Exactly halfway between them (in x). Every cubic is symmetric about its inflection point.

  3. Choose Quartic. Find a stretch where the top band is green (increasing) and the bottom band says ∩ (concave down). What is the graph doing there?

    What you should see

    Rising, but more and more slowly. That always happens just before a relative maximum, between an inflection point and the top.

  4. In the second lab, set the interval to [−0.5, 4.5]. Where are the absolute max and min?

    What you should see

    Both at the endpoints: f(4.5) ≈ 11.1 beats the relative max 5, and f(−0.5) ≈ −5.1 is below the relative min 1.

  5. Shrink the interval to [0.5, 3.5]. Now what?

    What you should see

    Now the turning points win: absolute max 5 at x = 1 and absolute min 1 at x = 3. Always check both the turning points and the ends.

Worked example: reading a cubic

For f(x) = x³ − 6x² + 9x + 1 (graphed), give the intervals where f increases and decreases, the relative extrema, the point of inflection, and the absolute extrema on [−0.5, 4.5].

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Increasing and decreasing. From the graph, f increases on (−∞, 1) and (3, ∞) and decreases on (1, 3).
  2. Relative extrema. Increasing then decreasing at x = 1: relative max f(1) = 1 − 6 + 9 + 1 = 5. Decreasing then increasing at x = 3: relative min f(3) = 27 − 54 + 27 + 1 = 1.
  3. Inflection. Concave down to the left of x = 2 and up to the right: inflection at (2, 3), halfway between the turning points.
  4. Absolute extrema on [−0.5, 4.5]. Compare f(−0.5) = −5.125, f(1) = 5, f(3) = 1, f(4.5) = 11.125. Absolute max 11.125 at x = 4.5, absolute min −5.125 at x = −0.5: both at the endpoints.

Worked example 2: the biggest box

Cut a square of side x cm from each corner of a 20 cm × 30 cm card and fold up the sides to make an open box. Write the volume V(x), give its domain, and use a graphing calculator to find the largest volume.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Dimensions. Height x, width 20 − 2x, length 30 − 2x. So V(x) = x(20 − 2x)(30 − 2x), a cubic.
  2. Domain. Every side must be positive: x > 0 and 20 − 2x > 0, so 0 < x < 10.
  3. Maximum. On (0, 10) the graph rises, turns and falls. The calculator gives the relative max at x ≈ 3.92 cm, with V ≈ 3.92 × 12.15 × 22.15 ≈ 1056 cm³.
  4. Check. V(2) = 2 × 16 × 26 = 832 and V(6) = 6 × 8 × 18 = 864, both less. On the open interval (0, 10) the turning point is the absolute maximum.

Common mistakes

  • Giving the x-value when asked for the maximum value (the output), or the other way around.
  • Forgetting the endpoints when finding an absolute extremum on a closed interval.
  • Calling every point where the graph looks flat a turning point: x³ flattens at 0 but keeps increasing.
  • Confusing a point of inflection (bend switches) with a turning point (direction switches).

Why at most n − 1 turning points

Each turning point is where the rate of change switches sign. For a polynomial of degree n, the rate of change behaves like a polynomial of degree n − 1 (you will see why in calculus), which has at most n − 1 zeros. So the direction can switch at most n − 1 times, and the bending at most n − 2 times.
