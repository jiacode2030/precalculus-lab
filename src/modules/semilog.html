Semi-log plots

On a log scale each step up the axis multiplies by 10 instead of adding 10. Exponential data, which curves on ordinary axes, becomes a straight line.

The idea

A semi-log plot has an ordinary x-axis and a logarithmic y-axis. Equal distances up the y-axis mean equal ratios (1, 10, 100, 1000), not equal differences. If y = a·bˣ, then log y = log a + x·log b, which is a line in x with slope log b and intercept log a. So data that is a straight line on a semi-log plot is exponential, and one that curves is not.

From the line you read the model. The y-intercept of the line is the starting value a (at x = 0). Each step of 1 in x multiplies y by b, so b = (y₂/y₁)^(1/(x₂ − x₁)) for two points. A line sloping upward means growth (b > 1), downward means decay (0 < b < 1). Take care: reading values off a log axis means reading powers of 10, so the gridlines are 1, 10, 100, 1000 and the marks between them (2, 3, … 9) bunch up toward the next power.

The mirror image is the logarithmic model y = a + b ln x. There you plot y against x on a log x-axis, and that is the straight line. Which axis to put on a log scale depends on which variable is "multiplicative". Scientists use semi-log plots for populations, sound levels, earthquake energy and drug concentrations, where values range over several powers of 10.

Straight or curved?

Exponential data is straight on a log y-axis. Predict, then look.

1. The points of y = 3·1.8ˣ are drawn on ordinary axes. Replot with a logarithmic y-axis: will they fall on a straight line?

Each step multiplies y by the same factor, so log y rises by the same amount each step: the points lie on a straight line.

2. The points of y = x², for x = 1 to 8 are drawn on ordinary axes. Replot with a logarithmic y-axis: will they fall on a straight line?

A polynomial grows by smaller and smaller factors, so log y bends (it curves, and flattens as x grows).

3. The points of y = 90·0.6ˣ are drawn on ordinary axes. Replot with a logarithmic y-axis: will they fall on a straight line?

Each step multiplies y by the same factor, so log y rises by the same amount each step: the points lie on a straight line.

4. The points of y = 5 + 12x are drawn on ordinary axes. Replot with a logarithmic y-axis: will they fall on a straight line?

Adding the same amount each step is a shrinking percentage change, so log y climbs fast and then flattens: it curves.

Worked example 2: a straight line on a log axis

The amount of a medicine in the blood (mg) after x hours is a straight line on a semi-log plot, passing through (2, 200) and (6, 12.5). Find the model, and when the amount falls to 1 mg.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. A line on a semi-log plot means y = a·bˣ. Use both points: 200 = a·b² and 12.5 = a·b⁶.
  2. Divide to get b. 12.5/200 = b⁴, so b⁴ = 1/16 and b = 1/2. The amount halves every hour.
  3. Find a. 200 = a(1/2)² = a/4, so a = 800. The model is y = 800(1/2)ˣ.
  4. Solve y = 1. 800(1/2)ˣ = 1, so (1/2)ˣ = 1/800 and x = ln 800 / ln 2 = 6.685/0.693 ≈ 9.64 hours. Check: 800/2^9.64 ≈ 1 ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the log axis like an ordinary one: the gridlines are powers of 10, not equal steps of 10.
  • Concluding that a curve is not exponential when only the y-axis was ordinary: only a straight line on a log y-axis proves exponential.
  • Forgetting that the line's slope is log b, not b.
  • Using the first y-reading as a (the start value) when the first point is not at x = 0.

Straight on the log axis: exponential. Straight on a log x-axis: logarithmic.

The two semi-log plots are the two ways around. If y = a·bˣ, plot y on a log axis against ordinary x. If y = a + b ln x, plot ordinary y against x on a log axis. If both axes are logarithmic (a log-log plot), a straight line means a power function y = a·xᵖ, which you will meet in calculus and science.
