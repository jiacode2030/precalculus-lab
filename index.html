    Sine, cosine & the unit circle

    An angle is a rotation. Turn a point around a circle of radius 1: its x-coordinate is the cosine and its y-coordinate is the sine.

    The idea

    Start at the point (1, 0) and rotate counterclockwise by an angle θ. Wherever you land on the unit circle (radius 1), the point is (cos θ, sin θ). That is the definition: no right triangle needed, so θ can be 125°, −30° or 7π.

    Angles are measured in radians: the length of arc the angle cuts on the unit circle. A full turn is 2π (about 6.28) radians, so 180° = π. On a circle of radius r the arc is r times longer: s = rθ.

    Every angle has a reference angle: the acute angle to the x-axis. The reference angle sets the sizes of sin and cos; the quadrant sets their signs. Learn quadrant I (0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2) and you know the whole circle.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Set the angle to 125°. Which coordinate is negative, and why does the tangent come out negative?

      What you should see

      The point is in quadrant II: x = cos 125° ≈ −0.574 is negative and y = sin 125° ≈ 0.819 is positive. tan = sin/cos ≈ −1.428, negative because the signs differ: the radius slopes down to the right.

    2. Turn snapping on and step through 30°, 150°, 210° and 330°. What stays the same, and what changes?

      What you should see

      All four have reference angle 30°, so |sin| = 1/2 and |cos| = √3/2 every time. Only the signs change: (+,+) in I, (−,+) in II, (−,−) in III and (+,−) in IV.

    3. Slide to 90°. What happens to tan θ, and what does the orange tangent line do?

      What you should see

      cos 90° = 0, so tan 90° = 1/0 is undefined. The radius is vertical, and the line through it never meets the vertical line x = 1: the tangent segment runs off to infinity.

    4. Set 30°, then 390°, then −330°. Compare the points.

      What you should see

      All three are the same point (√3/2, 1/2). Angles that differ by a full turn (360° = 2π) are coterminal, which is why sine and cosine repeat with period 2π.

    5. In the second lab, keep θ = 1 and drag the radius from 1 to 3. What happens to the arc length and to y/r?

      What you should see

      The arc length s = rθ triples (from 1 to 3), but y/r stays sin 1 ≈ 0.841. The ratio depends only on the angle, which is why sin θ = y/r works on a circle of any size.

    Worked example

    A bicycle wheel has radius 0.35 m. The tire valve starts level with the hub on the right (3 o'clock). The wheel turns 150° counterclockwise. (a) Where is the valve relative to the hub? (b) How far did the valve travel along the rim?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Convert the angle. 150° × π/180 = 5π/6 radians. It lands in quadrant II, with reference angle 30°.
    2. Height. On a circle of radius r the point is (r cos θ, r sin θ). y = 0.35 sin(5π/6) = 0.35 × 1/2 = 0.175 m above the hub.
    3. Width. x = 0.35 cos(5π/6) = 0.35 × (−√3/2) ≈ −0.303 m: 0.303 m to the left of the hub (the negative sign means left).
    4. (a) Answer. The valve is at (−0.303, 0.175) m relative to the hub: 0.303 m left and 0.175 m up.
    5. (b) Arc length. s = rθ = 0.35 × 5π/6 ≈ 0.916 m. The angle must be in radians for s = rθ.

    Worked example 2: exact values from the reference angle

    Find the exact values of sin(4π/3), cos(4π/3) and tan(4π/3).

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Locate the angle. 4π/3 = π + π/3: past the negative x-axis by π/3, so quadrant III.
    2. Reference angle. The acute angle to the x-axis is 4π/3 − π = π/3.
    3. Sizes from quadrant I. sin(π/3) = √3/2 and cos(π/3) = 1/2.
    4. Signs from the quadrant. In quadrant III both coordinates are negative: sin(4π/3) = −√3/2 and cos(4π/3) = −1/2.
    5. Tangent. tan = sin/cos = (−√3/2)/(−1/2) = √3. Positive, because the two negatives cancel (the radius slopes up to the right through the origin).

    Common mistakes

    • Mixing degrees and radians: sin(30) on a calculator in radian mode is sin of 30 radians, about −0.988, not 1/2.
    • Using the angle itself instead of the reference angle: sin(5π/6) is sin(π/6), not "sin of something bigger".
    • Getting the signs wrong: x is cosine (width) and y is sine (height). Quadrant II has negative cosine, positive sine.
    • Saying tan 90° = 0. It is undefined, because cos 90° = 0.
    • Using s = rθ with θ in degrees.

    Why the circle definition

    In a right triangle an angle can only be between 0° and 90°. Periodic things (a wheel, a tide, a sound wave) keep going around, so precalculus redefines sine and cosine as coordinates on the unit circle. For acute angles the two definitions agree: the radius is the hypotenuse (length 1), the height is the opposite side and the width is the adjacent side.

    Because the point is on the circle x² + y² = 1, every angle satisfies sin²θ + cos²θ = 1. That one fact is the Pythagorean theorem, and it gives one value from the other: if sin θ = 3/5 in quadrant II, then cos θ = −4/5.

    Sinusoidal transformations

    Every sine wave is the same wave, stretched and shifted. Four numbers say how: amplitude, period, phase shift and midline.

    The idea

    A sinusoidal function is any transformation of sine or cosine: f(θ) = a sin(b(θ + c)) + d. Each parameter does one job. a stretches vertically: the amplitude is |a|. d moves the wave up: the midline is y = d. b squeezes horizontally: the period is 2π/|b|. c slides it sideways: the phase shift is −c (so θ + π/4 moves the graph left by π/4).

    Cosine is a sine wave shifted left by π/2: cos θ = sin(θ + π/2). So a sinusoid can always be written either way; pick the one whose starting point is easiest (cosine starts at a maximum, sine starts on the midline going up).

    Read the parameters straight off a graph: midline = (max + min)/2, amplitude = (max − min)/2, period = the distance between two neighboring maxima.

    1. Set a = 2, then a = −2. What does a negative a do?

      What you should see

      The amplitude is still 2, but the wave is flipped over its midline: it now starts on the midline going down. Amplitude is |a|.

    2. Set b = 2 and then b = 0.5 (keep a = 1). How long is one cycle each time?

      What you should see

      Period = 2π/b: π ≈ 3.14 for b = 2 (twice as many waves) and 4π ≈ 12.57 for b = 0.5 (half as many). Bigger b squeezes the wave.

    3. Set b = 2 and c = 0.785 (about π/4). Is the shift π/4 or π/8? Read the start of the cycle.

      What you should see

      π/4 to the left. The form b(θ + c) factors b out first, so the shift is −c = −π/4. Had it been written sin(2θ + π/4) = sin(2(θ + π/8)), the shift would be π/8.

    4. Choose cos with c = 0, then sin with c = 1.57. Compare the two graphs.

      What you should see

      They match (up to rounding of π/2): sin(θ + π/2) = cos θ. A cosine is a sine that starts half a hump early.

    5. In the second lab, drag the minimum farther right. What happens to the period?

      What you should see

      The period is twice the horizontal distance from a maximum to the next minimum. Doubling that distance doubles the period and halves b = 2π/period.

    Worked example

    Find the amplitude, period, phase shift, midline and range of f(θ) = 3 sin(2(θ − π/4)) + 1, then sketch one cycle.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Midline. d = 1, so the midline is y = 1.
    2. Amplitude. a = 3, so the wave rises 3 above and falls 3 below the midline: range [−2, 4].
    3. Period. b = 2, so the period is 2π/2 = π.
    4. Phase shift. θ − π/4 means c = −π/4: the cycle starts π/4 to the right, at θ = π/4, on the midline going up.
    5. Key points. A quarter period later (θ = π/4 + π/4 = π/2) it reaches the max 4; at θ = 3π/4 it is back on the midline; at θ = π it hits the min −2; at 5π/4 the cycle ends.

    Worked example 2: the voltage in a wall outlet

    The voltage of a US wall outlet is modeled by V(t) = 170 sin(120πt) volts, with t in seconds. (a) Find the amplitude, period and frequency. (b) When is the voltage first 85 V?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Amplitude. a = 170: the voltage swings between −170 V and 170 V.
    2. Period and frequency. b = 120π, so the period is 2π/(120π) = 1/60 s and the frequency is 60 cycles per second (60 Hz).
    3. (b) Solve 170 sin(120πt) = 85. sin(120πt) = 1/2. The first positive angle with sine 1/2 is π/6, so 120πt = π/6 and t = 1/720 s ≈ 0.00139 s.
    4. Why "120 volts"? The label on an outlet is the root-mean-square value, peak ÷ √2: 170/√2 ≈ 120 V. The peak really is about 170 V (120√2 ≈ 169.7).

    Common mistakes

    • Reading the period as b instead of 2π/b.
    • Taking the phase shift from sin(2θ + π/2) as π/2. Factor first: sin(2(θ + π/4)), so the shift is π/4 to the left.
    • Getting the shift direction backwards: θ − h moves right by h, θ + h moves left.
    • Calling a negative a a negative amplitude. Amplitude is |a|; the sign flips the wave.
    • Forgetting the midline when finding max and min: max = d + |a|, min = d − |a|.

    Sine or cosine?

    Any sinusoid can be written with either. Cosine begins its cycle at a maximum, sine on the midline going up, so choose the one that matches an easy point on your graph or in your context. A Ferris wheel that starts at the bottom, for instance, is −cos: it starts at a minimum.

    Polar coordinates

    Instead of "over and up", say "how far and which way". A point in the plane can be named by its distance from the origin and its angle.

    The idea

    In polar coordinates a point is (r, θ): turn to the angle θ (counterclockwise from the positive x-axis), then go a distance r along that ray. It is how radar, sonar and a lighthouse beam describe position.

    Converting is the unit circle again, scaled by r: x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ. Going back, r² = x² + y² and tan θ = y/x, but tan θ = y/x has two solutions in each full turn: choose the angle in the quadrant where the point actually is.

    One point has many polar names. Adding 2π to θ lands on the same ray. A negative r means "walk backwards": (−r, θ) is the same point as (r, θ + π). A complex number a + bi is the point (a, b), so it has a polar form too: a + bi = r(cos θ + i sin θ).

    Worked example

    Write the point (−3, 3) in polar coordinates in two different ways.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Plot it. x = −3, y = 3: quadrant II.
    2. Distance. r = √((−3)² + 3²) = √18 = 3√2 ≈ 4.243.
    3. Angle. tan θ = 3/(−3) = −1. The quadrant II angle with tan θ = −1 is θ = 3π/4. (−π/4 also has tangent −1, but points into quadrant IV.) So the point is (3√2, 3π/4).
    4. A second name. Walk backwards along the opposite ray: (−3√2, 3π/4 − π) = (−3√2, −π/4). Check: x = −3√2 cos(−π/4) = −3 and y = −3√2 sin(−π/4) = 3.

    Worked example 2: a plane on the radar screen

    An air-traffic radar shows a small plane 12 km from the station at θ = 210°, measured counterclockwise from due east. How far east or west, and how far north or south, is the plane?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. The polar point. r = 12 km, θ = 210° = 7π/6: quadrant III, reference angle 30°.
    2. East–west. x = 12 cos 210° = 12 × (−√3/2) = −6√3 ≈ −10.39 km.
    3. North–south. y = 12 sin 210° = 12 × (−1/2) = −6 km.
    4. Answer. The plane is about 10.4 km west and 6 km south of the station. Check: √(10.39² + 6²) ≈ 12. Note that real navigation measures bearings clockwise from north; this problem uses the math convention.

    Common mistakes

    • Using θ = arctan(y/x) without checking the quadrant. For (−3, 3) the calculator gives −π/4, but the point is at 3π/4.
    • Thinking (r, θ) names a point uniquely. (r, θ), (r, θ + 2π) and (−r, θ + π) are all the same point.
    • Swapping sine and cosine: x goes with cos, y with sin.
    • Plotting a negative r on the ray θ. It goes on the opposite ray, θ + π.
    • Mixing degrees and radians within one problem.

    Converting equations

    The same substitutions turn a polar equation into a rectangular one. For r = 4 cos θ, multiply by r: r² = 4r cos θ, so x² + y² = 4x, which is (x − 2)² + y² = 4: a circle of radius 2 centered at (2, 0). Going the other way, the line y = 3 is r sin θ = 3, or r = 3 csc θ.

    Polar graphs

    Give a radius for every angle and a curve draws itself: roses, hearts, loops and spirals, all from one rule r = f(θ).

    The idea

    A polar function r = f(θ) sweeps a ray around the origin and, at each angle, puts a point at distance f(θ). Sweep θ from 0 to 2π and the points trace a curve. When f(θ) is negative, the point goes on the opposite ray.

    The same function has two pictures. In the (θ, r) plane it is an ordinary graph: θ across, r up. In the polar plane it wraps around the origin. Reading one from the other is the key skill: where the rectangular graph crosses zero, the polar curve passes through the origin; where |r| peaks, the polar curve is farthest out.

    Families to know: circles r = a, r = a cos θ, r = a sin θ; roses r = a cos(nθ) with n petals if n is odd and 2n if n is even; limaçons r = a + b cos θ (inner loop if |a| < |b|, cardioid if |a| = |b|, dimpled if |b| < |a| < 2|b|, convex if |a| ≥ 2|b|); spirals r = aθ.

    Worked example

    Sketch r = 2 + 4 cos θ for 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π. Where does it pass through the origin, and what is the inner loop?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Table of key angles. θ = 0, π/3, π/2, 2π/3, π give r = 6, 4, 2, 0, −2.
    2. Zeros. 2 + 4 cos θ = 0 when cos θ = −1/2: θ = 2π/3 and 4π/3. The curve passes through the origin at both angles.
    3. Negative r. For 2π/3 < θ < 4π/3, r < 0, so those points go on the opposite ray. At θ = π, r = −2 is plotted at (2, 0): this stretch makes the inner loop.
    4. Symmetry and shape. cos(−θ) = cos θ, so the curve is symmetric about the x-axis. |a| = 2 < |b| = 4: a limaçon with an inner loop, reaching out to (6, 0).

    Worked example 2: a microphone's pickup pattern

    A cardioid microphone picks up sound with relative sensitivity r = 1 + cos θ, where θ is the angle from the direction the microphone points. (a) Compare its sensitivity at the front, the side (θ = π/2) and the back. (b) At what angle is it 1.5, three-quarters of its maximum?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Front. θ = 0: r = 1 + 1 = 2, the maximum.
    2. Side. θ = π/2: r = 1 + 0 = 1, half the front value. Half the amplitude is 20 log₁₀(0.5) ≈ −6 dB, the "−6 dB at the side" printed on microphone spec sheets.
    3. Back. θ = π: r = 1 − 1 = 0. It hears nothing from directly behind, which is why singers point it at their mouth and away from the speakers.
    4. (b) 1 + cos θ = 1.5 gives cos θ = 0.5, so θ = π/3 (60°) on either side of the front.

    Common mistakes

    • Plotting a negative r on the ray θ itself instead of the opposite ray.
    • Counting petals of r = a cos(nθ) as n for every n: it is 2n when n is even.
    • Stopping at θ = π. Many curves need the full 0 to 2π (a rose with even n, any limaçon).
    • Reading the (θ, r) graph as if it were the shape. It is a different picture of the same function.
    • Assuming every limaçon has a loop: only |a| < |b| does.

    Reading the (θ, r) graph

    AP questions often show r = f(θ) on rectangular axes and ask about the polar curve. Three translations do most of the work: r = 0 on the graph means the curve is at the origin; |r| largest means the curve is farthest out; r negative means the point is on the opposite side of the origin from the angle θ.

    Rates of change in polar

    As θ turns, does the curve move toward the origin or away from it, and how fast? The answer is in r, not in x or y.

    The idea

    For a polar function r = f(θ), the average rate of change of r on [θ₁, θ₂] is (f(θ₂) − f(θ₁)) / (θ₂ − θ₁): how much the distance to the origin changes per radian of turning, on average. It is the slope of a secant on the (θ, r) graph.

    The sign of r and the direction r is changing together decide whether the point is getting closer to or farther from the origin. The distance is |r|. If r is positive and increasing, or negative and decreasing, |r| grows: farther. If r is positive and decreasing, or negative and increasing, |r| shrinks: closer.

    Where r changes from increasing to decreasing while positive, the curve is at a local maximum distance from the origin (the tip of a petal, the outer edge of a limaçon).

    4. Choose the sprinkler r = 10 + 4 cos θ. Compare the average rate of change on [0, π/2] and on [π, 3π/2].

      What you should see

      On [0, π/2]: (10 − 14)/(π/2) = −8/π ≈ −2.55 ft per radian (edge coming closer). On [π, 3π/2]: (10 − 6)/(π/2) = +8/π ≈ 2.55 (edge moving away).

    Worked example

    For r = f(θ) = 1 + 2 sin θ, find the average rate of change of r on [π/2, 7π/6], and say whether the curve is getting closer to or farther from the origin on that interval.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Endpoints. f(π/2) = 1 + 2(1) = 3 and f(7π/6) = 1 + 2(−1/2) = 0.
    2. Interval width. 7π/6 − π/2 = 7π/6 − 3π/6 = 2π/3.
    3. Average rate of change. (0 − 3)/(2π/3) = −9/(2π) ≈ −1.432: r drops about 1.43 units per radian, on average.
    4. Check the sign of r. On this interval sin θ > −1/2 (until 7π/6), so r > 0, and sin θ is decreasing, so r is decreasing.
    5. Conclusion. r is positive and decreasing, so the distance |r| shrinks: the curve gets closer to the origin, reaching it at θ = 7π/6.

    Worked example 2: how far the sprinkler reaches

    A lawn sprinkler waters a region whose edge is r = 10 + 4 cos θ feet from the sprinkler, θ in radians. (a) Find the average rate of change of r on [0, π/2] and interpret it. (b) Is the edge getting closer or farther on [π, 3π/2]?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Endpoints. r(0) = 10 + 4 = 14 ft; r(π/2) = 10 + 0 = 10 ft.
    2. (a) Rate. (10 − 14)/(π/2 − 0) = −4/(π/2) = −8/π ≈ −2.546 ft per radian.
    3. Interpretation. As the spray direction turns from θ = 0 to θ = π/2, the edge of the watered region gets closer to the sprinkler at an average of about 2.55 feet per radian of turning.
    4. (b) r(π) = 6 ft and r(3π/2) = 10 ft. r is positive and increasing, so the edge is getting farther: average rate (10 − 6)/(π/2) = 8/π ≈ +2.546 ft per radian.

    Common mistakes

    • Saying "r is decreasing, so the point gets closer" when r is negative. Then |r| is growing: it moves away.
    • Using the change in x or y instead of the change in r.
    • Forgetting the units: the rate is in units of r per radian.
    • Subtracting in one order on top and the other on the bottom.
    • Using degrees in the denominator: θ₂ − θ₁ must be in radians unless told otherwise.

    Why the sign of r matters

    The distance from the origin is |r|, not r. When r is negative, the point sits on the opposite ray at distance −r. If r goes from −1 to −3, it is decreasing, but the distance goes from 1 to 3: the point moves away. So always check two things: the sign of r and whether r is increasing or decreasing.
